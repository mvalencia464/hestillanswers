@@ PH01 | story | dusk | city | True story · February 11, 1861
H: BEFORE HE LEFT HOME|FOR THE LAST TIME,|LINCOLN ASKED HIS|NEIGHBORS *FOR ONE THING.*
C: Springfield, Illinois. February 11, 1861. Abraham Lincoln stood at the back of a train, about to leave for Washington and a country coming apart.

His neighbors had come to the depot to see him off. He spoke for barely two minutes.

"Here I have lived more than a quarter of a century; here my children were born, and here one of them lies buried. I know not how soon I shall see you again."

Then he told them what he was facing: a duty greater, perhaps, than any man had carried since Washington. And he told them he could not do it alone.

"I hope you, my friends, will all pray that I may receive that Divine assistance, without which I cannot succeed, but with which success is certain."

Not advice. Not votes. Prayer.

The train pulled out. He never saw Springfield again. Four years later it came back carrying his body.

Who is carrying something heavy right now and needs you to do what Lincoln's neighbors were asked to do?

@@ PH02 | story | dusk | city | True story · New York, 1857
H: HE RENTED A ROOM TO|PRAY AT LUNCH HOUR.|FOR THIRTY MINUTES|*NOBODY CAME.*
C: September 23, 1857. Jeremiah Lanphier, a businessman turned city missionary, had handed out flyers all over lower Manhattan inviting merchants and clerks to a noon prayer meeting in an upper room on Fulton Street.

At twelve o'clock he was the only one there.

Twelve-ten. Twelve-twenty. He prayed by himself.

At half past, he heard a step on the stairs. Then another. By one o'clock there were six men in the room.

The next week there were twenty. The week after, forty. They decided to meet every day.

That same autumn a financial panic closed banks and threw thousands out of work. The room filled. Then the building. Then other churches and theaters across the city. Within months, by the usual count, some ten thousand people were stopping to pray in New York every day at noon, and the movement was spreading city to city.

It began with one man who kept praying for thirty minutes in an empty room.

If you are in the empty-room part of your story, stay.

@@ PH03 | story | snow | tree | True story · Vermont, 1829
H: A THIEF DROVE OFF|WITH HIS SLEIGH.|HE PRAYED *FOR THE*|*THIEF'S CONSCIENCE.*
C: Marshfield, Vermont, about 1829. A man calling himself John Cotton had been in town six weeks, selling wooden clocks. He borrowed a neighbor's sleigh and harness for a three-day trip.

He did not come back. It turned out he had bought the clocks on credit, sold them all, and owed for his horse besides.

The owner felt it badly. They had little, and what they had came from hard labor. Chasing him was pointless.

About three weeks later he came in one evening, perfectly calm. "I shall not worry any more about my sleigh and harness. I think I shall get them again."

His wife asked why.

"I have been praying to God to arrest Cotton's conscience, so that he will be obliged to leave them where I can get them."

Six days later a letter came from an innkeeper forty miles off, in Littleton, New Hampshire. A man had ridden in at midnight, refused a bed, and insisted on leaving a sleigh and harness for a Mr. P. of Marshfield. He rode away at two in the morning on one of the coldest nights of the year.

He paid nobody else he owed. Only that.

@@ PH04 | story | storm | tree | Recorded 1885
H: THE TORNADO FLATTENED|EVERY TREE AROUND HER.|*EXCEPT THE ONE*|*SHE PRAYED FOR.*
C: The storm cut a path through the country that the old account puts at a mile wide. Trees were torn up by the roots. Barns and fences went flat.

Right in the middle of that path stood a small cabin. An elderly widow lived there with her only son.

In front of the cabin was one large tree. The wind caught it and began twisting and thrashing it. If it came down, it would come down on the house.

Everywhere around her, great trees were falling.

She could not hold up a tree. She could not move her house. So she did the one thing left. She knelt and asked the God who "gathereth the wind in his fists" to spare that tree.

When it was over, it was still standing.

It was the only tree left standing for a considerable distance around her cabin.

She did not ask Him to stop the storm. She asked Him for one tree.

Sometimes the prayer is not "take this away." It is "keep this one thing standing."
R: W

@@ PH05 | story | storm | church | True story · Indiana
H: THUNDER CRACKED AS HE|STOOD TO PREACH. HE SAID,|*"NOT ONE DROP WILL*|*FALL HERE TODAY."*
C: It was a camp meeting in southern Indiana, and it had rained nearly the whole week.

An old preacher they called Father Haven stood up and announced his text. Thunder answered him. The crowd shifted on the benches and looked at the sky.

"Let us engage a moment in prayer," he said.

He asked God to let the storm pass by and not disturb them. Then he looked up.

"Friends, keep your seats. It will not rain one drop here today."

He began to preach. It thundered again. He repeated what he had said. The cloud kept coming until it was nearly on top of them.

Then, the account says, it divided. Half went north and half went south, about a quarter of a mile off on either side. The two halves joined again beyond the camp and moved on.

"Not one solitary drop of rain fell on that encampment."

You can call it weather. The people sitting dry on those benches called it something else.

@@ PH06 | story | gold | cabin | True story · Plymouth, 1623
H: THE CORN WAS DYING.|THE PILGRIMS STOPPED|AND *PRAYED ALL DAY.*|BY NIGHT IT RAINED.
C: Spring, 1623. The Plymouth settlers planted more corn than they ever had. By the time the seed was in the ground, their food was gone.

They lived on what one boat and one net could catch. When the fish failed they dug shellfish out of the sand. Every day they prayed, "Give us this day our daily bread," and every day, somehow, there was something.

Then came drought. By June the corn was withering and the grass looked like hay. Without a harvest, they would starve.

So they set apart a day to humble themselves and pray. They kept at it for eight or nine hours.

The morning was clear and hot. So was the whole forenoon.

Before night it began to rain.

Not a cloudburst that would have beaten the stalks flat. Gentle showers, which kept falling for many days, until the ground was soaked and the drooping corn stood up again.

Afterward they set apart another day. That one was for giving thanks.
