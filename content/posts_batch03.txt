@@ B301 | story | sea-storm | ship | True story · Recorded 1885
H: HE STOOD ON DECK IN|THICK FOG HOLDING A|SUN INSTRUMENT.|*THE CREW JUST STARED.*
C: For days the ship had been wrapped in mist. No sun, no stars, no way to fix their position.

Captain Crossby went below and prayed. If he could trust God with his soul, he reasoned, he could trust Him with a ship. Then he asked for one specific thing: a clear sky at twelve o'clock, long enough to take a reading.

At eleven he came on deck with the quadrant under his coat. Drizzle. The men looked at him as if he had lost his mind. He went below and prayed again. Came up again. Nothing.

Ten minutes to twelve. Still gray.

Then, in the old account's words, the mist "seemed to be folded up and rolled away as by an omnipotent and invisible hand." The sun stood in a blue sky. His hands shook so badly he could barely take the reading.

They were on course. And the moment he finished, the mist rolled back over them and the drizzle began again.

He asked for noon. He got noon.

@@ B302 | story | sea-dusk | ship | True story · October 1876
H: FOR SIX DAYS HIS SHIP|*WOULD NOT MOVE.*|HE ASKED GOD WHY.|THEN HE REACHED PORT.
C: He had prayed before sailing from the Kennebec that October, and believed it was right to go.

Then, somewhere off the Bahamas, the ship simply stopped making way. They would sail what felt like thirty or forty miles and find by observation they had gained three or four. Day after day. The captain admitted it tried his faith. "Lord, why are we so hindered?"

At last they broke free and made port.

The first thing people on the dock asked was, "Where have you been through the gale?"

"What gale?"

A hurricane had torn across those waters, leaving wreck and ruin on either side of the patch of sea where he had sat fuming. One ordinary day of sailing would have put him in its path.

"We were anxious to sail on," he said, "but some unseen power held us where we were, and we escaped."

The delay you are praying against may be the answer to a prayer you never knew to pray.

@@ B303 | story | gold | cabin | True story · New York, 1830
H: HE CAME TO BUY A TREE.|HE BROUGHT *A BAG OF*|*FLOUR* AND COULDN'T|SAY WHY.
C: Central New York, about 1830. Grain was scarce and priced out of reach. In one farmhouse a mother of six sewed for wages as long as her eyes held out, and prayed that God would keep her children from want.

Everyone in the house knew the need. Nobody knew where the answer would come from. Each day the shelf got barer.

Four miles away, a man needed shingles. He decided to buy a pine off their land and split it himself. As he was leaving, a thought stopped him: they might be short of flour. He could not have known. He loaded two bushels anyway.

At the door he almost apologized for it. He had brought some flour toward the price of the tree, he said, in case they could use it.

"That is in answer to prayer," she told him.

Her husband was not a praying man. He believed it too.

When the oldest boy came in that night she had one thing to say: "God has answered our prayers, and sent a bag of flour."

@@ B304 | story | dawn | cabin | True story · The prairie
H: ONE PINT OF CORNMEAL|LEFT. HER LITTLE GIRL|*HOPED FOR POTATOES.*|THEN MORNING CAME.
C: A widow on the western prairie, four children, almost no work. One night there was a pint of cornmeal in the house and nothing else.

"Well, children," she said, "maybe the Lord will provide something."

"I do hope it will be a good mess of potatoes," said little Nell. "Seems to me I never was so hungry for taters before."

The mother lay awake, turning it over. Then, she said, the sweetest peace came over her and she slept.

In the morning she opened the door to go down to the brook. On the bench beside it stood pails of potatoes and a sack filled with flour.

She clapped her hands and shouted for the children, who came tumbling out half dressed, "like a flock of quails."

She washed the pails and set them back on the bench that night. By morning they were gone. She never learned who left them.

"The Lord never seemed so far off after that time," she said.

Nell asked for potatoes. Nell got potatoes.

@@ B305 | story | storm | church | True story · Oberlin, Ohio
H: THREE MONTHS, NO RAIN.|A SCOFFER MOCKED THE|PREACHER. *HE RAN HOME*|*AND FELL ON HIS KNEES.*
C: The fields around Oberlin were dying. For nearly three months every cloud that promised rain drifted out over Lake Erie and emptied itself there.

Charles Finney was out walking, watching another dark bank sit uselessly over the water, when a man named Ralph stopped him. How could Finney preach that God was always wise and always good, he demanded, while He poured rain on a lake and let them suffer for want of it?

Finney said the words cut him to the heart. He did not argue. He turned and ran home, shut himself in, and pleaded with God to answer for the honor of His own name.

"Before I rose from my knees," he said, "there was a sound of a rushing, mighty wind."

He looked out. The sky was black. The cloud was rolling in off the lake.

It rained in torrents for two full hours.

A professor who was there that day confirmed it years later. He remembered the cloud. He had been driven to his knees too.

@@ B306 | story | night | cabin | True story · Recorded 1885
H: THROWN OUT OF THE CITY|AT NIGHT, HE SAW ONE|LIGHT. INSIDE, A MAN|*WAS PRAYING FOR HIM.*
C: A Dutch preacher was holding a meeting in a city where he knew no one. Mid-sermon, a police officer walked in, shut it down, and ordered him out of town.

It was dark. He had nowhere to go. He wandered near the city gates, steadying himself with the words, "Lo, I am with you always."

Then he noticed a light in the distance and walked toward it. Perhaps, he thought, the Lord has a shelter for me there.

As he reached the house he heard a voice. A man was praying aloud. The preacher stopped at the door and listened.

"Lord Jesus, one of Thy persecuted servants may be wandering at this moment in a strange place of which he knows nothing. May he find my home, that he may receive here food and lodging."

He waited for the "Amen." Then he stepped inside.

The two men dropped to their knees together.

Someone may be praying for you tonight who does not even know your name.

@@ B307 | story | sea-dusk | ship | True story · Liverpool
H: HER TRUNKS WERE|ALREADY ON THE SHIP.|MID-PRAYER HE STOPPED:|*"YOU MUST NOT GO."*
C: A young woman in Liverpool had booked passage to join her family in Jamaica. Her luggage was aboard. The ship sailed in the morning.

Before leaving she called on her minister, William Bramwell, to say goodbye and ask for his prayers. They knelt, and he began commending her to God's care for the voyage.

Then he went quiet.

"My dear sister, you must not go tomorrow. God has just told me you must not go."

She was stunned. Everything was arranged. But he would not be moved, and at last she gave in. He went down to the docks himself and helped carry her trunks back off the vessel.

The ship sailed without her.

About six weeks later the news reached Liverpool. It had gone down. Everyone on board was lost.

Not every closed door is a punishment. Some of them are a rescue.

Which closed door are you thankful for now?

@@ B308 | story | night | city | True story · London
H: SPURGEON'S COLLEGE|WAS OUT OF MONEY.|A FRIEND SAID,|*"OPEN YOUR LETTERS."*
C: Charles Spurgeon told this one himself, from his own pulpit.

His college for training young ministers had been running about a year when the money ran out. One Sunday evening he paid away the last he had.

"There is nothing left, whatever," he told a friend.

"You have a good banker, sir."

"Yes, and I should like to draw upon Him now, for I have nothing."

"Have you prayed about it?" He had. "Then leave it with Him. Have you opened your letters?"

Spurgeon did not open mail on Sundays. "Open them for once," said his friend.

The first envelope was from a bank. A lady, completely unknown to them, had left two hundred pounds for him to use in educating young men.

"Such a sum has never come since, and it never came before," Spurgeon said. He never found out who she was. "To me it seemed that it came directly from God."

The answer was already on his desk.

@@ B309 | story | gold | church | True story · Bavaria
H: HE GAVE A BEGGAR HIS|LAST THREE COINS. THEN|HE PRAYED, *"LORD, I LENT*|*THEM TO YOU."*
C: Michael Feneberg was the pastor of Seeg, in Bavaria. One day a traveler with an empty purse begged him for three crowns to finish his journey.

It was all the money Feneberg had. But the man asked in the name of Jesus, so in the name of Jesus he gave it.

Almost at once he found himself in real need, with no way out. So he prayed the way a child talks to a father:

"Lord, I lent Thee three crowns. Thou hast not yet returned them, and Thou knowest how I need them. Lord, I pray Thee, give them back."

That same day a letter arrived with money in it. The traveler had told his story to a rich man, who sent a gift for the generous vicar.

Two hundred thalers. For three crowns.

The old pastor laughed out loud in amazement. "Ah, dear Lord, one dare ask nothing of Thee, for straightway Thou makest one feel so much ashamed!"

You cannot out-give God. People keep trying.

C: Nettie lived in an attic with a mother too sick to work. There was no bread. So she knelt by the bed and said, slowly, "Give us this day our daily bread."

Then she went out to find where God kept it.

Around the corner was a baker's shop with full shelves. This must be the place, she thought. She walked in.

"I've come for it."

"Come for what?"

"My daily bread. I'll take two, if you please. One for mother and one for me."

He bagged them. She turned to go. "Stop, you little rogue! Where is your money?"

She had none. He called her a thief. She burst into tears and told him everything: her mother, the prayer, how she thought God meant her to come and fetch it.

The baker was a rough man. He was not a hard one.

"You poor, dear girl. Here, take this to your mother." He filled a large basket and put it in her arms.

She asked for two loaves. She went home with a basketful.

C: A young minister and his wife were sent to their first charge in Vermont around 1846. Few members, most of them poor. Within months the couple had cooked the last food in the house.

They ate that final breakfast and prayed together. Then he went out to an old barn to lay it before God, and she did the same in her room.

The minister told this story himself until he died in 1875. Then his wife kept telling it.

C: Samuel Hick, a Yorkshire blacksmith, had promised two loads of corn for a church gathering at Micklefield. The day was close, there was no flour in the house, and the country had been becalmed so long that every windmill stood with its arms out, catching nothing.

Sammy carried his corn to the nearest mill anyway and asked the miller to unfurl his sails.

"There's no wind."

"I will go and pray while you spread the cloth."

The miller did it mostly to humor him. The canvas was barely stretched when a fine breeze came up. The sails spun. The corn became meal. Sammy shouldered it and went home rejoicing.

A neighbor had seen the mill turning and hurried over with corn of his own. Too late. The air was dead again.

The miller shrugged. "You must send for Sammy Hick to pray for the wind to blow again."

God is not short of wind. He may be waiting for someone to spread the sails.

C: Admiral Sir Thomas Williams was crossing the Atlantic when Ascension Island showed as a smudge on the horizon. It was uninhabited then. Ships went there for turtles and nothing else.

As he looked at it, he was seized by a desire he could not account for: steer toward it.

He knew how that would sound to his crew, so he tried to ignore it. It only grew stronger. Finally he told his lieutenant to put the ship about.

The officer objected, respectfully. It would cost them time. The men were just sitting down to dinner. Could it not wait?

The arguments only made the captain more anxious. The ship turned.

Every spyglass on deck was soon trained on the shore. "It is white. It is a flag. It must be a signal!"

Sixteen men had been wrecked there many days before. They were starving. They had put up the flag almost without hope that anyone would ever see it.

Someone did. He just did not know why he was looking.

C: Two brothers were climbing in the Monte Rosa range. Near the top they walked into dense fog.

The guides suddenly turned and planted their axes. One brother, seeing what looked like more slope ahead, pushed on past them. He did not know he was walking onto a cornice, a lip of snow hanging over a drop of several thousand feet.

The guides screamed.

He stood for a moment on the very edge. Then the snow gave way and he began to go through.

One guide lunged after him, at the risk of his own life, caught him by the coat, and dragged him back.

The man was a minister. When he got home, an invalid in his congregation told him she had been praying for his safety, and that at one particular hour she had felt driven to plead for him as if he were in terrible danger.

They compared the times.

They matched.

You may never know, this side of heaven, what your prayers held back.

@@ B316 | story | night | church | True story · Recorded 1885
H: SHE HAD NO SHOES FOR|CHURCH, SO SHE PRAYED.|*A YOUNG TEACHER RODE*|*TWO MILES THAT DAY.*
C: She was a widow with an invalid son, and she had stopped going to church and to the neighborhood prayer meeting. Not for lack of wanting to. For lack of shoes.

So she asked the Lord for a pair.

That very day the village schoolmaster stopped in to see her boy. He noticed her shoes. He said nothing. But he could not shake it.

He hired a horse, rode two miles to a shoe store, bought a pair, and asked that they be sent to her cottage without delay.

They fit perfectly.

That night she hurried to the prayer meeting to tell everyone that the Lord had sent her shoes.

The young schoolmaster was sitting in the room. He heard every word. He went home, the account says, and wet his pillow with tears of gratitude that God had used him to answer a widow's prayer.

He was an old, white-haired man when he finally told the story.

You might be the answer to a prayer you will hear about tonight.

@@ B317 | story | dusk | cabin | As told by D. L. Moody
H: THE LANDLORD SAID,|"GET OUT." HER LITTLE|GIRL WENT TO ASK GOD|*FOR A HOME.*
C: D. L. Moody used to tell about a family at the start of the Civil War. The father had joined the army. A few days later the landlord came for the rent, and the mother did not have it.

He stormed. They would have to leave. He was not going to keep people who could not pay.

When he was gone she dropped into a chair and wept.

Her little girl came over. "What makes you cry, mamma? I will pray to God to give us a little home, and won't He?"

What could her mother say? The child went into the next room. The door was open.

"O God, you have come and taken away father, and mamma has got no money, and the landlord will turn us out, and we will have to sit on the doorstep, and mamma will catch cold. Give us a little home."

A pause. Then: "Won't you, please, God?"

She came out perfectly happy.

The landlord's heart changed. Moody said that mother never paid rent again.

B: Take the helmet from my brow. Let me rest a while with You.
C: A prayer for the one taking care of everyone else.

Lord, I am tired in a way sleep does not fix.
I have been strong for so long that I have forgotten how to be anything else.
Take the helmet from my brow. Lift the weight of this armor for a while.
Let me sit down in Your presence and just have peace.
You see every unnoticed thing I do. That is enough for me.
Give me strength for tomorrow, tomorrow.
Tonight, give me rest.
In Jesus' name, amen.
